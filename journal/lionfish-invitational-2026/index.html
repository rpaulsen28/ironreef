Hunting Lionfish at the Flower Garden Banks: Diving the 2026 Lionfish Invitational With My Dad

By Ryan Villanueva, PADI Divemaster and IGRA competitor · November 16, 2026

This past July my dad, Michael Paulson, and I spent four days on a liveaboard in the Gulf, about a hundred miles off the Texas coast, hunting invasive lionfish for science. It was the Lionfish Invitational, and it was one of the best dive trips I've ever done.

Dad kept a running journal the whole trip. This post is built from his notes, with his permission, and told from my side of the mask.

Why lionfish?

Lionfish are beautiful, and they don't belong here. They're native to the Indo-Pacific, but they've spread through the Atlantic, the Caribbean and the Gulf. They eat huge numbers of young reef fish and have almost nothing here that eats them. The Invitational puts trained divers in the water to remove as many as possible and to collect them for researchers, who measure and study every fish that comes back to the boat.

Sunday: 3 AM and a long drive

We left Kansas City at 3 AM, with me driving, and made it to Clute, Texas, in time for the orientation dinner. We met the crew and the research team and sat through presentations on the boat, the diving, and how the lionfish survey and collection would work.

We boarded the MV Fling at 7:45 that night for loading, a boat orientation, a safety briefing and a food briefing. The bunks are all forward, eight of them; Dad was in #4 and I was in #6. Storage space is tiny, but the berths are comfortable and each has its own curtain. We were asleep by 10:30.

Monday: the Flower Garden Banks

We were up at 6:15 and tied to a mooring ball at West Flower Garden Bank by 6:35.

Dive 1, West Bank, buoy 1. The research team ran survey lines north, south, east and west, and each buddy team got assigned a sector. Ours was the southeast. Lots of barracuda and great reef life. Dad got our first lionfish at about 80 feet: a clean head shot. The water was around 86°F, and Dad, in a full sharkskin and cap, was way too warm.

Dive 2, same site. This time we worked the northwest quadrant. The diver ahead of me on the line had a tank problem, and I sorted it out before we went on. Dad spotted a lionfish and I speared it, another clean shot. After each dive, every fish got measured for the researchers.

Dive 3, East Bank, buoy 5. A new site at 5:30 in the afternoon.

Dive 4, East Bank night dive. Dad and I love night dives. They're calmer, and both of us breathe less air at night. Then a strong current came up and swept six divers off the marker lines and away from the boat. When the dinghy went after them, its motor died, and the driver had to haul it back to the Fling by hand. The Fling dropped the mooring and went to pick everyone up. Everyone was fine, but it was a good reminder of why you always hold the line in current.

Tuesday: oil rig day

Rig dive 1, High Island A-379B. This is an active oil rig. We tied off to the north side, with a tag line from the bow to the rig at 30 feet. From there you can drop to whatever depth you're planned for, up to 130 feet on this dive. Every metal surface was covered in sponges, crustaceans and things that sting. We saw jellyfish and lots of barracuda, and fought very strong current. Most of the lionfish were below 100 feet. Dad and I stayed between 87 and 95, and fighting the current ate our air, so we were back up in just under 30 minutes.

Rig dive 2, High Island A-570A. This was a cut-off rig with the tie-in at 92 feet, which meant a short dive. The rig is big enough that divers were split into two teams, A and B (pronounced "Bey," as the crew put it). Dad and I were on Team A. Because of the stinging life, full body coverage and gloves were required: I wore my BARE wetsuit and Dad wore sharkskin top and bottom.

This was the best dive of the trip. Coral covered everything. It's an invasive coral that grows on metal, not something anyone wants, but it's everywhere on Gulf rigs. And fireworms: we counted a group of 15 in about one square foot.

During the surface interval, a pod of dolphins rode alongside the boat for ten minutes or more, and the whole boat came up on deck to watch.

Team Bey had the next rig, High Island 571, to themselves, so we sat that one out.

Rig dive 3, High Island 515. A collapsed rig lying on its side, sloping down into deep water, tied off at 92 feet. Some current, but nothing like that morning. We didn't see any lionfish ourselves, though others collected a few. I set a new personal depth record of 121 feet, and Dad, of course, went to 123.

Wednesday: Stetson Bank, one more rig and the Kraken

Dive 1, Stetson Bank, buoy 2. Stetson is a spot where the bottom pushes up to within 80 or 90 feet of the surface, and it looks like a moonscape: long ridges of shale with valleys in between. Not much coral grows there, but the fish life was great: groupers, eels and scorpionfish. People also saw several sharks and an adult manta ray. Dad got a nice 37.5 cm lionfish. It took him four shots to land it and a fifth to get it into the Zookeeper, the tube we store lionfish in underwater.

Dive 2, High Island A-474A. We were the last team in, so we watched the water until the first science divers came back. We dropped to the tie-off at 32 feet, where the first platform sits, and passed the second platform at 74 feet. We worked our way around most of the rig and found one lionfish at 88 feet, which Dad collected. There were huge numbers of very large sergeant majors and two big loggerhead turtles.

Dive 3, the Kraken. The Kraken is a wreck in more than 90 feet of water. We tied to the bow and went down the starboard side, staying between about 100 and 118 feet. There were lots of sea cucumbers and less coral, more algae. It was a short dive because of the depth and the number of dives we'd already logged.

Dive 4, the Kraken, stern tie-in. Dad sat the last dive out, so I joined another group. I had one shot at a lionfish, but it was deep, and I was getting close to my decompression limit. Rather than push it, I let my teammate take the shot. Going into deco means you have to stay under longer to off-gas nitrogen before you can safely surface. That's bad for the boat's schedule, and it raises your risk of decompression sickness. On the fourth dive of a deep day, it's not worth one fish.

Why we don't eat this catch

Lionfish are great eating in most places, but not the ones from this trip. The Flower Garden Banks area is a known hot spot for ciguatera, a toxin made by tiny algae that builds up the food chain through the reef fish and shrimp that lionfish eat. The research team told us more than a quarter of the lionfish they've tested from here carried it. In people, it mostly causes stomach and intestinal symptoms. So everything we collected went to science, not the grill.

Trivia, and the ride home

With the diving done, we rinsed gear, ate dinner and finished the trip with team trivia, where no topic was off limits. The Fling headed back overnight, and we were in Freeport by 7 the next morning.

Diving with my dad, doing real conservation work, and hitting a depth record on the same trip? That's a hard one to top. If you're thinking about a trip like this, or wondering whether you're ready for deep rig and wreck diving, that's exactly what my dive consulting is for.
