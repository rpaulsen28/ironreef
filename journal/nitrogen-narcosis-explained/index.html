Nitrogen Narcosis, Explained (and Why Divers Joke About Martinis)

By Ryan Villanueva, PADI Divemaster and IGRA competitor · November 3, 2026

Ask a group of divers about nitrogen narcosis and somebody will mention martinis. The joke is old, the effect is real, and understanding it makes you a safer, calmer diver. Here's the plain-English version.

What it is

Nitrogen narcosis is a reversible change in how your brain works when you breathe nitrogen at higher pressure. Air is mostly nitrogen. At the surface it does nothing noticeable, but as you descend, the pressure rises and the nitrogen starts to act a little like an anesthetic. Your thinking slows down, your judgment gets fuzzy, and some divers feel a light, giddy buzz.

The martini joke

The classic way to explain it is "Martini's Law": every 33 feet (10 meters) of depth feels like having another martini on an empty stomach. Divers love it because it's memorable, and because it captures the most important point: you don't feel impaired, you feel fine, while your reasoning quietly gets worse. Treat it as a rule of thumb, not a measurement. How much it affects you changes from person to person and from day to day.

That joke is the idea behind our Every 33 Feet Martini Tee: a martini glass full of seawater, with a depth gauge at 33 feet.

How deep does it show up?

Most divers don't notice narcosis on shallow dives. It usually becomes noticeable somewhere around 100 feet (30 meters) on air, and the effects grow the deeper you go. That's one reason recreational training limits depth: entry-level divers are typically trained to about 60 feet (18 meters), deeper certifications go further, and most recreational agencies set an upper limit of about 130 feet (40 meters).

Things that can make it worse include cold water, poor visibility, fatigue, stress, a hard workload, alcohol the night before, and certain medications.

What it feels like

  • Slower thinking and trouble with simple math (like reading your gauge or working out your turn pressure)
  • Overconfidence, or the opposite: a sudden wave of anxiety
  • Fixating on one thing and losing track of your buddy, depth or air
  • Giggling, euphoria or a "tipsy" feeling

What to do about it

The good news: narcosis goes away when you ascend. Coming up a few meters usually clears it within minutes, with no lasting effects. So the fix is simple:

  1. Know your plan before you descend. Depth, time and turn pressure should be decided on the boat, not worked out at depth.
  2. Check in with yourself and your buddy. If either of you seems off, signal and ascend a few meters together.
  3. Stay within your training and experience. Build depth gradually with an instructor or a deep diver course.

Does nitrox prevent narcosis?

A lot of divers believe nitrox makes them feel clearer at depth. Controlled studies haven't shown that nitrox reduces narcosis, because oxygen at pressure is also thought to be narcotic. Nitrox has real benefits, but narcosis protection isn't one you should count on. More in Nitrox for new divers.

This article is general information, not dive training. Learn and practice deep diving with a certified instructor, and follow the limits of your training and your dive plan.
